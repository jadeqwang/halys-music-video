# How did Thales foretell the eclipse of 28 May 585 BC?

Research note for S53, 4 Oct 2026. Tags: **[C]** corroborated (primary text, our own ephemeris run, or settled scholarship) · **[P]** plausible but speculative · **[U]** unsupported.

**How it was checked.** The egress proxy blocks almost every scholarly host (JSTOR, SAGE, Springer, Wiley, arXiv, Wikipedia, Perseus, NASA, university servers). Primary texts were read in full from the Perseus and First1KGreek TEI files on GitHub. Secondary literature was checked through search-engine extracts of the publisher pages linked in the sources; points that rest on extracts alone are marked *(extract)*. Eclipse circumstances are our own runs: JPL DE422 in Skyfield 1.55 with the NASA-canon ΔT. The setup was validated by reproducing RESEARCH §1.4 for 585 BC: greatest eclipse near 38°N 45°W at 14:22 UT, Moon/Sun 1.0798, and 0.971 at Miletus.

## Verdict

- **[C]** Herodotus (c. 430 BC) says Thales foretold the darkness to the Ionians, setting *this year* as the limit. Later writers repeat that he predicted it. **No ancient source says how.**
- **[C] Context.** In Thales' lifetime, Babylonian scholars held eclipse records going back to 747 BC. They flagged lunar-eclipse *possibilities* by counting months: 6 apart, sometimes 5. They used the 223-month cycle for lunar eclipses by about 575–550 BC. Nobody anywhere could predict where a solar eclipse would be seen.
- **[P]** Some count of lunar months between past eclipses could have given him a year in which an eclipse was possible: the 223-month cycle from the eclipse of 18 May 603 BC, Couprie's "clusters", or O'Grady's 23½ months. On that view, he was then lucky that it was total over Lydia. Pure luck, or a story improved after the event, is equally plausible.
- **[U]** These are all unsupported: geometry as the method; the 1/720 as the method; "he used the saros" as fact; any forecast of the day, hour or place; and an Egyptian method.

**Q1, answered.** No ancient source describes a method. The nearest claims are about the cause of eclipses and the Sun's size (§6), and neither gives a date.

## 2. What existed in his lifetime: Babylon

| Practice | Attested | Lunar / solar | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Systematic eclipse records | From 747 BC. A large compilation in 18-year groups covered 747–315 BC; its earliest surviving entry is 731 BC. It already includes predicted eclipses, but it survives only in later copies. | Lunar | Steele 2000a; Brack-Bernsen & Steele 2005 *(extract)* |
| Astronomical Diaries | The earliest dated diary is from 652 BC | Both | Steele 2000a *(extract)* |
| Court scholars forecast eclipses and watch for them | 7th c. BC, under the Assyrian kings. The reports separate expected eclipses, eclipses watched for that did not occur, and eclipses hidden by weather. One report, as restored, reads "the moon and the sun, passed". | Lunar; solar *possibilities* | Steele 2026 *(extract)*; Parpola, SAA 10 |
| Possibilities 6 months apart, sometimes 5 | Listed for every year of Nebuchadnezzar II, 604–576 BC, including predicted eclipses marked "omitted" | Lunar | LBAT 1420 = BM 38462 (Hunger, ADART V, no. 6) |
| 223-month cycle: 38 possibilities, 33 six-month steps plus 5 five-month steps | "At least by the middle of the sixth century BC" (Steele & Stephenson 1997); "almost certainly before about 575 BC" (Brack-Bernsen & Steele 2005) *(extracts)*. It began with lunar eclipses and was applied to solar ones later. | Lunar first | as cited |
| "Saros Canon" (BM 34597 = LBAT 1428) and the other Saros Cycle Texts | Late Babylonian (Seleucid) copies. They tabulate lunar-eclipse possibilities in 223-month cycles; one survey dates the Canon's entries to 400–271 BC. | Lunar (3 of the 4 texts) | Aaboe et al. 1991 *(extract)* |
| Mathematical astronomy (Systems A and B) | From about 400 BC | Both | Ossendrijver 2012 *(extract)* |
| Saying *where* a solar eclipse would be seen | **Never.** All 61 Late Babylonian solar predictions concern eclipses visible somewhere on Earth, often far from Babylon. | Solar | Steele 1997; Neugebauer 1957 |

Neugebauer (*The Exact Sciences in Antiquity*, 2nd ed. 1957): "there exists no cycle for solar eclipses visible at a given place: all modern cycles concern the earth as a whole. No Babylonian theory for predicting a solar eclipse existed at 600 B.C., as one can see from the very unsatisfactory situation 400 years later, nor did the Babylonians ever develop any theory which took the influence of geographical latitude into account." *(extract)*

**The name.** Edmond Halley named the 223-month cycle "saros" in *Philosophical Transactions* in 1691. He took the word from a corrupt entry in the Byzantine *Suda*; Babylonian *šār* means 3,600. Le Gentil objected in 1756 (IMCCE 2019) *(extract)*.

**Q2a, answered.** By 600 BC, counting months to find lunar-eclipse possibilities is attested. The 223-month cycle was almost certainly in use by about 575 BC, and by about 550 BC at the latest. Solar-eclipse possibilities were watched for. Local visibility could not be predicted by anyone.

## 3. The eclipse of 18 May 603 BC (our computation)

585 BC falls exactly one 223-month cycle (6,585 days) after 18 May 603 BC. Both are in NASA's Saros 57, and both have a Moon/Sun ratio of 1.0797. Central line (DE422, ΔT 18,661 s), all times UT:

- **05:20** sunrise in central Africa (4°N 14°E), then Sudan, the Red Sea and Arabia
- **06:15** across the northern Persian Gulf (28.5°N 50°E), then southern and central Iran
- **06:50** greatest eclipse in Turkmenistan (about 38°N 62°E), with nearly 6 minutes of totality (5 m 52 s in our run)
- **08:25** sunset near Sakhalin, after Kazakhstan, Mongolia and Lake Baikal

| Place | Max magnitude | Local solar time | Sun altitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Miletus / Sardis | 0.50 / 0.50 | 08:08 / 08:13 | 36° / 37° |
| Athens | 0.45 | 07:53 | 33° |
| Halys bend | 0.59 | 08:46 | 43° |
| Memphis / Sais | 0.69 / 0.66 | 08:13 | 37° |
| Jerusalem | 0.71 | 08:35 | 42° |
| **Babylon** | **0.85** | 09:21 | 52° |
| Ecbatana / Susa | 0.88 / 0.93 | 09:47 / 09:40 | 56° |

With ΔT ±1,000 s (about ±2σ), Miletus ranges from 0.44 to 0.56 and Babylon from 0.78 to 0.93. In Ionia the Moon covered half the Sun's width in mid-morning, which is easy to miss unless someone is looking; in Babylon it was deep. **Two figures that circulate online fail.** "0.83 at Miletus" cannot be reproduced with standard ΔT. "Not visible in Egypt or the Babylonian observation cities" (IEP) is true of totality only: partial phases of 0.66–0.85 were visible in both places. To "look again 223 months later", Thales would have needed a record from further east and knowledge of the cycle. Neither is attested for him **[P]**.

## 4. Egyptian sources

They contain nothing on predicting eclipses **[U]**. No Egyptian eclipse method is known from the period. The earliest plainly written Egyptian eclipse records are demotic papyri (P. Berlin 13146 + 13147) of about the 1st century BC *(extract)*. The demotic eclipse-omen book (P. Vienna D 6278+, a 2nd-c. AD copy) is Mesopotamian material adapted in Egypt (Parker 1959) *(extract)*. Where ancient writers link Thales with Egypt (DL 1.24, 1.27; Plutarch), the subject is geometry and the pyramids, not eclipses.

## 5. Modern hypotheses

| Hypothesis | Proponents | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 223-month cycle counted from a recorded eclipse (603 BC) | The traditional view; still "strong opinion" according to O'Grady's IEP article | **[P]** It needs Babylonian solar data and a way for it to reach Thales; neither is attested. It gives a possibility, not visibility (Neugebauer). |
| 54-year triple cycle (*exeligmos*) | Panchenko 1994, pointing to 21 Sep 582 or 16 Mar 581 BC | **[U]** for the battle. Stephenson & Fatoohi 1997: the 582 track "passed far to the south of Asia Minor; the magnitude at any point in the peninsula cannot have exceeded 85%" *(extract)*. Our run gives 0.80 at Miletus. |
| No period explains it; he meant 18 May 584 BC | Hartner 1969 tested 15 cycles (as summarised by Querejeta 2011) | **[P/U]** In our run, 584 reached only 0.32 at Miletus. |
| 23½ months after a lunar eclipse | van der Waerden; O'Grady 2002 | **[P]**, weak. Couprie 2004 puts its success rate at no more than 23 %. |
| Clusters of three eclipses seen at Miletus: the second 17 or 18 months after the first, the third at 35 months | Couprie 2004 and 2011; Dubal 2025 (preprint) | **[P]** The pattern is real in Miletus' sky. Our scan finds 30 Sep 610, 13 Feb 608 and 30 Jul 607, then 29 Jul 588, 14 Dec 587 and 28 May 585, each spaced 17 + 18 lunations; after 585 it breaks, as Couprie says. It requires partial eclipses of 0.6–0.9 to have been noticed and remembered. |
| Luck, or a story improved after the event | Neugebauer 1957. Dicks 1959: "he could not possibly have possessed this knowledge which neither the Egyptians nor the Babylonians nor his immediate successors possessed" *(extract)*. | Geometry | No scholarly proponent found | **[U]** Predicting by geometry needs sizes, distances and parallax. Anaxagoras explained eclipses in the 5th century; Hipparchus (2nd c. BC) and Ptolemy (*Almagest* VI, c. AD 150) computed them for particular places. |

**Names in the brief.** *Graham & Hintz* (Apeiron 40, 2007) is about Anaxagoras and the eclipse of 478 BC, not Thales. *Couprie* exists and argued as described. *Mosshammer*: some summaries also credit him with a "clusters" idea, which I could not confirm from his text.

## 6. The 1/720 claim

- **Diogenes Laertius 1.24** (Hicks): "according to some the first to declare the size of the sun to be one seven hundred and twentieth part of the solar circle, and the size of the moon to be the same fraction of the lunar circle." The Greek ends ἀπεφήνατο κατά τινας ("according to some"). The words for "of the solar circle, as also the size of the moon" are an editor's supplement, marked 〈…〉 in Hicks' Greek.
- **Apuleius, *Florida* 18** (2nd c. AD), the earliest report of any measurement of the Sun by Thales: "idem sane iam procliui senectute diuinam rationem de sole commentus est … quoties sol magnitudine sua circulum quem permeat metiatur". In English: in his declining old age he worked out "how many times the sun measures by its own size the circle it travels". He gives no number, and **old age** means decades after 585.
- **Archimedes, *Sand-Reckoner* 1** (3rd c. BC): Ἀριστάρχου μὲν εὑρηκότος … τὸν ἅλιον φαινόμενον ὡς τὸ εἰκοστὸν καὶ ἑπτακοσιοστόν. He credits Aristarchus with finding that the Sun appears about 1/720 of the zodiac circle, and knows no measurement by Thales. Heath (1913) and Graham (2013) doubt the Thales attribution (as reported by Cuadra 2023).
- **Q3, answered: it has no relevance to prediction.** The figure is an angular size, ½°. It explains why the Moon can just cover the Sun, and says nothing about *when*. As a method it is **[U]**.

## 7. Recommendation for S53

S53 runs 183.34–187.65. In the lyric, "foretold" falls at 183.35, "sun" at 184.51 and "dark" at 186.38.

**What the overlay may show**

- Thales as the marble/painted figure, looking up.
- Gold lines for things later sources credit him with, dim and unlabelled, as ornament only: the triangle in a semicircle (DL 1.24–25) and the shadow stick (Plutarch 147A; DL 1.27). Never caption them as the method.
- **Replace the `1⁄720` payoff with the year.** Upper right, draw a gold arc of twelve new-moon ticks (a year of months, no numbers), closed at each end by a short boundary stroke for Herodotus' οὖρος, the "limit". On "sun" the Sun lights inside the arc; on "dark" the Moon's disk covers it. If less code is wanted, keep the two disks closing on "dark" but delete both `1⁄720` labels and the 720-part rulings.
- **PLAQUE**, lower third under the carved lyric, from 184.51 to 187.65 and held dim through S54: `HE NAMED ONLY THE YEAR`. That is 22 characters in 3.1 s, about 7 cps. An optional small second line, `HERODOTUS 1.74`, brings it to 36 characters, about 12 cps. If the director would rather address "how", the alternative is `NO SOURCE SAYS HOW` (18 characters).

**What it must not show:** a month count from 603; "223"; a table of past eclipses; a ring of 18 years; a gnomon presented as the eclipse tool; degrees; the word "saros"; or anything that puts the eclipse on a day or over a place.

**The two existing elements**

- **v1's `SAROS · 18 Y 11 D 8 H` ring stays gone.** The figure is correct modern astronomy, but around Thales it claims he used the cycle, which is unattested, and it uses Halley's 1691 name. If the cycle is wanted, it belongs in S64, where the Antikythera dial really is a 223-month spiral, with no link to Thales.
- **The `1⁄720` payoff goes.** It is not a prediction method. Its attribution is hedged ("according to some"), late (Apuleius, 2nd c. AD), partly an editor's supplement, and placed by Apuleius in Thales' old age, after 585. Archimedes gives the value to Aristarchus. Set on "foretold … dark", it reads as how he did it.
