# HALYS: historical research and visual reference

> Companion to `TREATMENT.md`, `ZEITGEIST.md` and `refs/INDEX.md` (48 public-domain or own-computed references). Facts are tagged **[V]** verified (primary text or our own ephemeris run), **[S]** scholarly consensus, **[?]** disputed. Version 1, 3 Oct 2026.

## 0. Ten things the art team must know

1. **It was late afternoon, not midday.** Totality fell at about **18:20 local solar time**, with the Sun **8–10° above the WNW horizon**, under an hour before sunset. Shadows were 6–7 times the height of whatever cast them, pointing ESE. The light after totality was real golden hour. (§1.4)
2. **The clock.** About **56 minutes** of partial phase, about **13 minutes** of deep dimming, then **2–3½ minutes of totality** (3 m 25 s on the centre line). The darkness came **out of the sunset at about 18,000 km/h.** (§1.4, §5)
3. **Jupiter, not Venus.** The one brilliant "star" was **Jupiter (−1.8), 11° above and 5.5° left of the black Sun.**
   - **Venus was below the horizon.** Fix the pre-chorus beat.
   - Pollux and Castor stood about 19° above the Sun. No Orion, no Sirius. (§1.6)
4. **The horizon burns all round, brightest to the NNE and SSW.** The corona looks smaller and warmer than in modern photographs, because it is seen through about 6½ air masses. Its shape is our call. (§5)
5. **Nobody knows where the battle was.**
   - Herodotus never says "at the Halys"; the Halys is the treaty border.
   - Totality certainly swept **Sardis, the Konya–Aksaray plain and Tarsus**.
   - The **Halys itself saw a 96–99 % partial eclipse** (by diameter) unless ΔT was about 300 s larger than the standard value, which is well within the uncertainty.
   - The magnitude-0.6 figure online is wrong: it belongs to the 610 and 603 BC eclipses. (§1.3, §1.5)
6. **Thales was about 40, not an old sage.** Give him a gnomon, tally tablets and a bowl of water. **No telescopes, astrolabes or armillary spheres.** (§2.3, §3.1)
7. **Medes:** soft rounded felt caps or hoods, sleeved knee-length tunics, **trousers**, a short sword strapped to the **right thigh**, composite bows with combined bow-case and quiver, wicker shields. **Not** the fluted Persian crown or robe of Persepolis.

   **Lydians:** Greek-like bronze, **crested helmets, lion blazons, long hair**, and **long-spear cavalry** (Herodotus 1.79).

   **Tell them apart by costume, never by faces.** (§3)
8. **No stirrups, no framed saddles, no horseshoes.** Saddlecloths only. Median horses wear **topknots and knotted tails.** (§3.5, §3.7)
9. **The peace had outsiders and blood in it.**
   - A Babylonian and a Cilician mediated, so they get a third and fourth costume language.
   - The oath was sworn by **cutting the arms and licking each other's blood**.
   - A **royal wedding** sealed it. (§2.4–2.7)
10. **Our no-blue palette has a classical pedigree.**
    - Greek master painters used **four colours, no blue** (Pliny).
    - Their prized "**Sinopian red**" was mined **in Cappadocia**, the Halys country (Strabo).
    - The river itself is **red-brown and brackish**. (§6, §1.3)

### 1.1 What Herodotus actually says (*Histories* 1.73–74)

The translation is A. D. Godley (Loeb, 1920), in the lightly modernised Perseus text. The Greek was checked against the Perseus edition. **[V]**

> **1.74.1** "…there was war between the Lydians and the Medes for five years; each won many victories over the other, and once they fought a battle by night."
>
> **1.74.2** "They were still warring with equal success, when it happened, at an encounter which occurred in the sixth year, that **during the battle the day was suddenly turned to night.** Thales of Miletus had foretold this loss of daylight to the Ionians, fixing it within the year in which the change did indeed happen."
>
> **1.74.3** "So when the Lydians and Medes saw the day turned to night, **they stopped fighting, and both were the more eager to make peace.** Those who reconciled them were Syennesis the Cilician and Labynetus the Babylonian;"
>
> **1.74.4** "they brought it about that there should be a sworn agreement and a compact of marriage between them: they judged that Alyattes should give his daughter Aryenis to Astyages, son of Cyaxares; for without strong constraint agreements will not keep their force."
>
> **1.74.5** "These nations make sworn compacts as do the Greeks; and besides, **when they cut the skin of their arms, they lick each other's blood.**"

**Key Greek phrases**

- τὴν ἡμέρην ἐξαπίνης νύκτα γενέσθαι: "the day suddenly became night."
- οὖρον προθέμενος ἐνιαυτὸν τοῦτον: Thales "set this year as the limit." The prediction was only to the year: a vague forecast, not a time of day.

**Cause of the war (1.73)**

- A band of Scythians in Cyaxares' service were insulted by him. They served him a murdered boy as game and fled to Alyattes at Sardis.
- Alyattes refused to hand them over.
- So the war began as an asylum dispute with fault on both sides. Neither king is the villain in Herodotus.

**What Herodotus does *not* say** (keep captions honest)

- **He never says the battle was fought at the Halys.** "Battle of the Halys" is a modern label.
  - He says the Halys was the boundary of the two empires (1.72).
  - He says Cyaxares ruled "all of Asia that is beyond the river Halys" (1.103). **[V]**
  - The battlefield is unknown. **[?]**
- He does not use the word "eclipse". He does not say the armies panicked. He says they **stopped and grew eager for peace.**
- Herodotus gives no numbers, terrain, time of day, or positions for the armies.
- He wrote about 430 BC, roughly 155 years after the event.

### 1.2 The date: 28 May 585 BC **[V][S]**

**The date**

- **28 May 585 BC** in the Julian calendar (astronomers' year −584). That is **22 May** in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.
- Season: about five weeks before the summer solstice. Our computed solstice falls on Julian 29 June of that year.

**Only one eclipse fits.** We computed every solar eclipse visible at Sardis, Miletus and the Halys bend from 620 to 560 BC (JPL DE422, NASA-style ΔT).

This agrees with Stephenson & Fatoohi, *JHA* 28 (1997) 279–282.

**Ancient dates for the event**

- Pliny, *Natural History* 2.53: Thales predicted the eclipse "in the fourth year of the 48th Olympiad… in the 170th year of the City" (≈ 585/584 BC). **[V]**
- Eudemus, quoted by Clement (*Stromata* 1.65): "about the 50th Olympiad." This is loose.

**Why "584 BC" keeps appearing**

- Astronomers write the year as −584, because there is no year zero.
- Pliny's Olympic year 48.4 ran from midsummer 585 to midsummer 584 BC. A May inside it would be May 584.
- AUC 170 is 584 BC.
- The sky settles it: there was no total or near-total eclipse over Anatolia in 584 BC.

**History of the identification**

- 19th-century astronomers argued between 610 and 585 BC. Airy (1853) championed 585.
- Modern lunar theory and ΔT studies confirm it.
- A minority of scholars doubt the prediction story, or that the eclipse coincided with a battle at all. **[?]**

### 1.3 The place

**The river**

- The Halys is today's **Kızılırmak**, Turkish for "Red River". The Hittites called it *Maraššantiya*.
- It is 1,355 km long, the **longest river entirely within Turkey**.
- It rises in the east near Sivas, sweeps west-south-west through Cappadocia in a great arc, then turns north to the Black Sea near Bafra.
- Strabo derives "Halys" from *halai*, the salt-works it flows past (12.3.12, 12.3.39). **[V]**
  - The river really is brackish. Gypsum and halite in its upper basin give it electrical conductivity of 1,100–5,200 µS/cm.
  - Its red-brown colour comes from iron-rich clay. Avanos potters still use river clay.

**The battlefield is unknown [?]**

The totality band ran 225–230 km wide from WNW to ESE across Anatolia. Under both standard ΔT models it passed over:

- Smyrna and **Sardis** (Alyattes' own capital)
- Phrygia south of Gordion
- the Konya plain
- the south shore of Tuz Gölü, Aksaray and Tyana
- **Tarsus**, in mediator Syennesis' Cilicia

It then ran into Syria at sunset. See `refs/eclipse/computed_eclipse-path-585BC-anatolia_DE422.png`. **[V]**

The Halys arc itself lay just *north-east* of the band (§1.5). A battle anywhere on the plateau inside the band (Lycaonia, Cappadocia, south-east Phrygia) would have had 2–3½ minutes of night.

**Headline numbers**

- **Time of day: late afternoon, about 18:20 local solar time, under an hour before sunset.** Not midday.
  - Sardis had totality at about 17:59. Miletus saw a 97 % partial phase at about 17:56.
  - At the eastern end of the plateau (Kerkenes, Kültepe, the Avanos bend), **the Sun set while still eclipsed.**
- **Sun altitude: about 8–10° above the WNW horizon** (azimuth ~289°).
  - Shadows were 6–7 times as long as the objects casting them and pointed ESE (azimuth ~109°).
- **Totality:** **3 m 25 s on the centre line** east of Konya (C2 18:17 local solar time, Sun at 9.1°). 2–3 minutes over most of the band; zero at the edges. **2 m 16 s** at the representative site.
- **Deep dimming** (≥90 % covered) lasted about 13 minutes. The whole partial phase before totality took about 56 minutes. That is the film's countdown clock.
- **Path width:** 225–230 km over Anatolia, measured perpendicular to the track. About 270 km at greatest eclipse.
- **Shadow speed:** about 4–6 km/s (15,000–22,000 km/h) over central Anatolia, heading ESE (113°). **The darkness came out of the sunset**, from the WNW.
- **Moon vs Sun size:** apparent diameters of 33.5′ vs 31.4′ (ratio 1.066). The Moon over-covers the Sun by about 1′ all round, so the inner corona and prominences show best near C2 and C3.
- **Greatest eclipse** (NASA *Five Millennium Canon*, via search snapshot; the NASA site is blocked here):
  - 19:28:50 TD = 14:22:26 UT, magnitude 1.0798, Saros 57, maximum duration about 6 m 04 s, over the mid-Atlantic.
  - Our run: 14:22:40 UT near 38°N 45°W; 6 m 04.5 s; band about 270 km wide there.

### 1.5 ΔT and the "was it really total at the battlefield?" debate **[V][?]**

**What ΔT is**

- ΔT = TT − UT, the accumulated slowing of Earth's spin. In 585 BC it was about 5.1 hours.
- Every **240 s of ΔT moves the path 1° of longitude.**
- The 1σ uncertainty in 585 BC is about **±460 s** (Morrison & Stephenson 2004 error model).

**The two standard models**

| Model | ΔT |
|---|---|
| NASA canon / Espenak–Meeus (Morrison–Stephenson 2004 parabola, lunar-corrected) | **18,384 s** |
| Stephenson–Morrison–Hohenkerk spline (2016; 2020 update, as built into Skyfield) | **18,213 s** |

**Maximum magnitude along the Halys** (our sweep; * = total, with duration)

| ΔT (s) | Avanos bend | Hirfanlı | Kırıkkale | Kalecik | Kerkenes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 17,400 | 0.950 | 0.948 | 0.933 | 0.927 | 0.920 |
| 18,213 | 0.984 | 0.981 | 0.966 | 0.960 | 0.953 |
| 18,384 | 0.991 | 0.988 | 0.972 | 0.967 | 0.960 |
| 18,800 | **1.008*** 2 m 08 s | **1.004*** 1 m 36 s | 0.989 | 0.983 | 0.977 |
| 19,400 | **1.031*** 3 m 30 s | **1.027*** 3 m 30 s | **1.012*** 2 m 42 s | **1.006*** 1 m 58 s | 1.000 |

**Reading the table**

- With either standard ΔT, the Halys saw **98–99 % at its southern bend and 96–97 % farther north** (magnitude, i.e. fraction of the Sun's diameter covered): a deep, eerie dimming, but **not night.**
- The southern bend enters totality from ΔT ≈ 18,700 s, about 0.7σ above NASA. Most of the arc is total by about 19,400 s.
- We could **not** reproduce the "magnitude ~0.6 at the Halys" figure that circulates online (ZEITGEIST §2.3) for any ΔT from 17,400 to 19,800 s.
  - Magnitudes of 0.6–0.7 at the Halys belong to the *rival* eclipses of 610 and 603 BC. Please correct ZEITGEIST.

**Defensible phrasing for the film**

- "Totality swept central Anatolia."
- "Whether it reached the Halys itself depends on ΔT."
- The terminal gag of dragging the ΔT band onto the battlefield (TREATMENT v0.1) is a **real** scientific degree of freedom.

### 1.6 The sky at totality **[V]**

From 38.4°N 33.9°E at 15:58 UT. See `refs/eclipse/computed_sky-at-totality-585BC-looking-WNW.png`.

**The Sun**

- The black Sun sits at altitude 8.4°, azimuth 289° (WNW), **in Gemini**. The Moon is in Gemini too.

**Planets**

- **Jupiter, magnitude −1.8**, is the one dazzling point near the Sun.
  - Altitude 19°, azimuth 284°: **11° above and 5.5° to the left (south) of the Sun**, 12° away.
- Mars (+1.8): altitude 31°, azimuth 278°, up and to the left.
- Saturn (+1.2): high in the south-west, altitude 61°.
- Mercury (+2.8): 15° from the Sun. Too faint to notice.

**Venus was below the horizon** (altitude −31°; it was a morning star that spring). **The TREATMENT's "Venus appears" should read "Jupiter appears."** This is an easy, defensible correction.

**Stars probably visible** (magnitude ≤ 1.2; how many depends on sky brightness)

- **Pollux (+1.1), about 19° straight above the Sun, with Castor (+1.6) just to its right. The Twins stand over the dead Sun.**
- Procyon (+0.4), to the left at altitude 19°.
- Capella (+0.1), low in the WNW–NW glow at altitude 6°.
- Regulus (+1.4), WSW at altitude 54°.
- Behind the viewer: Arcturus (−0.1) high in the east; Vega low in the north-east; Spica in the south-south-east; Antares low in the ESE.

**Not in the sky:** Sirius, Orion, Aldebaran and the Pleiades were all below the horizon. **Do not paint Orion.**

**The pole:** no pole star. Kochab was 7° from the celestial pole and Polaris 15°. Thales was credited with teaching sailors to steer by the Little Bear (Callimachus, in Diogenes Laertius 1.23). **[V]**

### 1.7 Season and weather **[S]**

**Season**

- Late May on the plateau (900–1,100 m) is green steppe starting to yellow, with poppies and grain still green in the fields.
- **May is central Anatolia's wettest month**: about 50–60 mm and about 9 rain days at Ankara. Afternoon cumulus and thunderheads are plausible.
- Days are warm (low 20s °C) and evenings cool.

**Daylight**

- Sunrise about 04:45 and sunset about 19:13, local solar time.
- After totality the Sun hung 0–8° up for about 50 minutes: **a genuine golden hour.** The TREATMENT's "warriors walk home along the river at golden hour" is astronomically exact.

## 2. People

### 2.1 Alyattes, king of Lydia (r. c. 610–560 BC) **[S]**

**Family and capital**

- Mermnad dynasty: son of Sadyattes, great-grandson of Gyges (Herodotus 1.16).
- Capital: **Sardis**, under Mount Tmolus on the gold-bearing Pactolus. Herodotus mentions "the gold dust that comes down from Tmolus" (1.93).

**Reign**

- 57 years according to Herodotus (1.25). Conventionally c. 610–560 BC; scholars' dates differ by a decade or two.

**What he did** (1.16–22, 1.25) **[V]**

- Drove the Cimmerians out of Asia.
- Took Smyrna; its destruction about 600 BC is archaeologically confirmed.
- Attacked Clazomenae.
- Inherited his father's war on Miletus and fought it in a peculiar way: he burned the crops every year but left the farmhouses standing, so the Milesians would replant (1.17).
- Ended that war and became "friends and allies" with Miletus' tyrant Thrasybulus. Thales lived in Thrasybulus' Miletus (Diogenes Laertius 1.27).
- In thanks for recovering from an illness, sent Delphi a great silver bowl on a welded-iron stand by Glaucus of Chios.

**Money**

- Herodotus says the Lydians were "the first men whom we know who coined and used gold and silver currency" (1.94).
- The early electrum lion-head coins belong to his era. Some carry the Lydian legend **WALWET, read as "Alyattes"**.

**Household**

- Croesus was his son by a **Carian** wife; Croesus was 35 at his accession c. 560 BC, so born c. 595 (1.26).
- Pantaleon was his son by an **Ionian** wife (1.92).
- His daughter was **Aryenis**.
- In short, a cosmopolitan court of Carian and Ionian wives, Greek craftsmen and Scythian refugees.

**Age in 585 BC: unknown.** Probably 40s–50s. Designer-safe: about 50.

**Tomb**

- Herodotus calls it the greatest work in Lydia, "except those of Egypt and Babylon" (1.93).
- It is the Koca Mutaf Tepe tumulus at Bin Tepe, north of Sardis: about 355 m across and 60–69 m high (sources differ).

**Character cues from the sources:** pragmatic, patient, honour-bound. He refused to surrender suppliants.

### 2.2 Cyaxares, king of the Medes (r. c. 625–585 BC) **[S]**

**Family and capital**

- Son of Phraortes, grandson of Deioces.
- Capital: **Ecbatana**, modern Hamadan in western Iran. Herodotus' legendary seven concentric walls have battlements of white, black, purple, blue and orange, then silver and gold (1.98).
- The Medes were an **ancient Iranian people**. Herodotus says they were once called "Arians" (7.62).

**Reign**

- 40 years "including the Scythian domination" (1.107). Conventionally c. 625–585 BC.

**What he did** **[V]**

- He "first organized the men of Asia in companies and posted each arm apart, **the spearmen and archers and cavalry**: before this they were all mingled together in confusion" (1.103).
- He had Scythian refugees teach Median boys "their language and the skill of archery" (1.73).
- With Nabopolassar of Babylon he destroyed **Nineveh in 612 BC**, 27 years before the eclipse, ending the Assyrian empire. The Babylonian Chronicle calls him Umakištar. **[S]**

**Character cues**

- Herodotus calls him "prone to anger" (1.73).
- He is also the founder-soldier who turned a federation of tribes into a great power.

**Age in 585 BC: unknown.** His father died c. 625, so Cyaxares was probably 55–65. Designer-safe: about 60, at the end of a long reign. On the conventional chronology he **died within a year of the battle**, and Astyages succeeded in 585/584. **[?]**

### 2.3 Thales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 546 BC) **[V][?]**

**Age in 585 BC: about 39, not an old sage.**

- Apollodorus, in Diogenes Laertius 1.37–38, says Thales died in the 58th Olympiad (548–545 BC) aged 78, which implies a birth year of about 624.
- The surviving manuscripts actually read "35th Olympiad" (640 BC), which would make him about 55. Sosicrates gives 90 years.
- Designer-safe: **40–50.**

**Background**

- Son of Examyas and Cleobulina, of a family Herodotus calls "Phoenician by descent" (1.170). "Most writers represent him as a genuine Milesian and of a distinguished family" (DL 1.22).
- The first man called one of the **Seven Sages**.
- Eudemus' *History of Astronomy* (in DL 1.23) says he was "the first to predict eclipses of the sun and to fix the solstices."

**Verified anecdotes**

- **The olive presses** (Aristotle, *Politics* 1259a, tr. Rackham):
  - He was "taunted with the uselessness of philosophy" for his poverty.
  - "From his knowledge of astronomy he had observed while it was still winter that there was going to be a large crop of olives." He paid deposits on "the whole of the olive-presses in Miletus and Chios" at low rent.
  - At harvest he let them "out on what terms he liked," "proving that it is easy for philosophers to be rich if they choose, but this is not what they care about."
  - This is the "first options trade."
- **The well** (Plato, *Theaetetus* 174a, tr. Fowler): "While he was studying the stars and looking upwards, he fell into a pit, and a neat, witty Thracian servant girl jeered at him… because he was so eager to know the things in the sky that he could not see what was there before him at his very feet." Diogenes Laertius (1.34) has an old woman and a ditch instead.
- **All is water** (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 983b): "the permanent entity is water (which is why he also propounded that the earth floats on water)." He also held that "the world is animate and full of divinities" and that magnets and amber have souls (DL 1.24, 1.27).
- **The pyramids by shadow** (DL 1.27, after Hieronymus): "he measured the height of the pyramids by the shadow they cast, taking the observation at the hour when our shadow is of the same length as ourselves."
- **His theorem** (Pamphila in DL 1.24): "he was the first to inscribe a right-angled triangle in a circle, whereupon he sacrificed an ox." This is the angle in a semicircle. Proclus, citing Eudemus, adds four more theorems: a diameter bisects a circle; the base angles of an isosceles triangle are equal; vertical angles are equal; and two triangles with equal angle-side-angle are congruent.
- **Sky and calendar:** credited with the Little Bear as a guide for sailors, two treatises (*On the Solstice*, *On the Equinox*) and a 365-day year (DL 1.23, 1.27).
- **Statesman:** he urged the Ionian cities to form a single council at Teos (Herodotus 1.170), and talked Miletus out of an alliance with Croesus (DL 1.25).
- **Back at the Halys in 547 BC.** The Greeks believed Thales got Croesus' army across the Halys. He dug "a deep **crescent-shaped** trench" (μηνοειδέα, literally "moon-shaped"), so the river split in two and both branches could be forded (Herodotus 1.75; DL 1.38). Herodotus himself thought there were bridges. **[V]**
- **Death:** while watching athletic games, "from heat, thirst, and the weakness incident to advanced age" (DL 1.39).
- **His statue's epigram:** "Pride of Miletus and Ionian lands, / Wisest astronomer, here Thales stands" (DL 1.34).
- **Likeness:** none survives. The one herm inscribed with his name, in the Vatican, has lost its head. The design is ours to make.

**How could he have predicted it? [?]**

1. Herodotus only claims a forecast *to the year*.
2. **Babylonian** scribes kept eclipse records for centuries and could flag eclipse *possibilities* with period rules: six-month spacing, and the 223-month "saros" for lunar eclipses by at least the mid-6th century BC. They could say when an eclipse was possible *somewhere*, not whether it would be seen *here*.
3. The 585 eclipse falls exactly one saros after the **18 May 603 BC** eclipse, which we compute as a morning partial of about 0.5 at Miletus. "Look again 18 years on" is conceivable.
4. **Sceptics:** Neugebauer wrote that "there exists no cycle for solar eclipses visible at a given place." Many historians think Thales was lucky, or that the story grew afterwards.
5. **Designer-safe:** show him *counting*: tally rows of months, a list of past eclipse dates, a gnomon's shadow. Show no instrument he could not have had.

### 2.4 Syennesis "the Cilician" **[?]**

- "Syennesis" recurs for different Cilician rulers: Herodotus 1.74 (585 BC), 5.118 (c. 497), 7.98 (480), and Xenophon's *Anabasis* 1.2 (401 BC). It is probably a **dynastic title**. The man of 585 is otherwise unknown.
- His country was Cilicia (Assyrian Hilakku and Que), with its capital at **Tarsus**.
  - Its culture was Luwian (Neo-Hittite) mixed with Assyrian, Phoenician and Greek. The bilingual Luwian–Phoenician inscriptions of Karatepe date from about 700 BC.
- A neutral power on the southern flank of both empires.
- **Tarsus lay inside the 585 BC totality band:** about 3 m 15 s, with the Sun 7° up (our run).

### 2.5 Labynetus "the Babylonian" **[?]**

- "Labynetus" is Herodotus' rendering of **Nabû-naʾid (Nabonidus)**.
- In 585 BC Babylon's king was **Nebuchadnezzar II** (605–562).
- The mediator was either Nebuchadnezzar under a garbled name, or, as many prefer, **the future king Nabonidus** (r. 556–539) serving as Babylon's envoy, then perhaps in his 30s.
- Babylon had been Media's partner in destroying Assyria. Here it acts as peace-broker on the Anatolian front.
- **Resonances**, all true of Nabonidus:
  - Lifelong devotion to **Sîn, the Moon god**. His mother was a votary of Sîn at Harran.
  - His stele in the British Museum shows him with a staff before the **crescent Moon, the Sun disc and the star of Venus**.
  - He was the father of **Belshazzar**, of "the writing on the wall."

### 2.6 Aryenis and Astyages: the peace marriage **[V][?]**

**Aryenis**

- Daughter of Alyattes, sister or half-sister of Croesus. She is **known only from Herodotus 1.74**.
- Age: probably mid-teens to early 20s. Her later life is unrecorded.

**Astyages**

- Son of Cyaxares. Babylonian sources call him *Ištumegu*.
- King c. 585–550 BC: 35 years in Herodotus (1.130).
- Overthrown by his own grandson **Cyrus**. The Nabonidus Chronicle records that his army mutinied and handed him over (c. 550/549).
- His daughter Mandane, Cyrus' mother, was probably born *before* 585, so he was likely already middle-aged. Designer-safe: 35–45.
- Herodotus' later stories of his cruelty belong to a different film. Do not foreshadow them.

**Why the marriage matters later.** It made Croesus Astyages' brother-in-law. That bond was Croesus' motive to attack Cyrus in 547 (1.73.1–2). **[V]**

### 2.7 The blood oath (Herodotus 1.74.5) **[V]**

- "These nations make sworn compacts as do the Greeks; and besides, **when they cut the skin of their arms, they lick each other's blood**."
- Greek: ἐπεὰν τοὺς βραχίονας ἐπιτάμωνται ἐς τὴν ὁμοχροίην, τὸ αἷμα ἀναλείχουσι ἀλλήλων.
- Herodotus ascribes the custom to **both** peoples.
- **For the screen:** the TREATMENT's midstream forearm clasp is a dignified abstraction. The closest faithful version that is not grotesque:
  - A shallow cut on each king's forearm.
  - Forearms pressed together.
  - The drops fall into the red river.

### 2.8 Aftermath **[V][S]**

- **585–547 BC:** the Halys is the frontier for a generation (Herodotus 1.72).
- **c. 550 BC:** Cyrus deposes Astyages.
- **547/546 BC** (traditional date; the chronicle entry is disputed):
  - Croesus asks Delphi and is told that if he sends an army against the Persians "he would destroy a great empire" (1.53).
  - Aristotle gives the famous form: "**Croesus, by crossing the Halys, shall ruin a mighty dominion**" (*Rhetoric* 1407a; Κροῖσος Ἅλυν διαβὰς μεγάλην ἀρχὴν καταλύσει).
  - He crosses the Halys (by Thales' crescent channel, the Greeks said).
  - He sacks Pteria, probably the burned city on **Kerkenes Dağ**, and fights Cyrus to a draw.
  - Sardis falls after a fourteen-day siege. "Fulfilling the oracle, he had destroyed his own great empire" (1.86).
- **Archaeology of the end:**
  - Sardis' mid-6th-century destruction layer has soldiers' skeletons, bronze arrowheads and an iron helmet with bronze trim found in 1987 (Greenewalt & Heywood, *BASOR* 285, 1992).
  - It is the last stand of the army in our film, 38 years later.

## 3. Character briefs for image generation

### 3.0 Rules for every sheet

1. **Equal dignity.** Every Lydian hero beat gets a Median mirror beat with the same light, the same closeness, the same finish of costume.
   - Leaders on both sides wear comparable gold.
   - Ranks on both sides are disciplined, individual and handsome.
   - Neither side is ever faceless, masked, or lit from below.
2. **Differentiate by costume, never by face or skin.**
   - Lydians (Anatolian) and Medes (Iranian) were neighbouring West Asian peoples. Give both olive to light-brown skin, dark hair and eyes, and natural variety in age, build, and the occasional hazel eye or greying beard.
   - Readability keys for a mêlée:
     - **Lydians = bronze and crimson**: crested bronze helmets, round painted shields, long hair.
     - **Medes = felt and saffron**: soft rounded caps or hoods, sleeved tunics, trousers, bows, wicker shields.
3. **Palette (Act I colour script): umber, ochre, vermilion, bronze, lead white. No blue.**
   - Real Medes and Babylonians used indigo and lapis; we deliberately hold blue back for the Earth reveal.
   - Dyes of the period: madder red, kermes crimson, murex purple (render as deep maroon), weld or saffron yellow, undyed wool and felt, tan and dark leather.
   - Metals: bronze (warm brown-gold); iron (dark, matte, rusty); gold (Lydian electrum is a paler gold).
4. **Period no-gos (all sheets):**
   - Riding: stirrups, framed saddles, horseshoes. Riders sit on saddlecloths.
   - Metal: steel or chrome shine, chain mail, plate armour.
   - Weapons: scimitars and sabres, crossbows.
   - Wrong eras: Roman kit (scutum, segmented armour, togas, laurel-wreathed emperors); turbans, keffiyehs, Islamic-era ornament.
   - Persian court material: the fluted Persian crown, the Faravahar.
   - Symbols and flags: lion-and-sun or any modern national symbol; flags of any kind. Animal standards on poles are fine.
5. **Sheet prompt header** (prepend to each seed below):
   > *Character turnaround sheet: front, three-quarter, profile and back, full body plus head close-up and prop details; neutral warm-grey background; museum-accurate archaeological costume of c. 585 BC Anatolia; painterly realism with a single soft key light from upper left; small labelled colour swatches; no other lettering.*

## 4. Set briefs

### 4.1 The Halys battlefield: central Anatolian plateau, late May, about 17:30–19:15 local solar time

**Where to put it.** Herodotus gives no site. Two defensible choices:

| Option | What it buys | Honesty note |
|---|---|---|
| **A. The Halys reach that flows toward the sunset**: from Avanos past Gülşehir to Hirfanlı (Kesikköprü), the river runs **W to WNW** (271–314° on Natural Earth geometry). That is within a few degrees of the eclipsed Sun's azimuth (289°). | **Perfect mirror symmetry:** camera looks *downstream* into the WNW. The red river runs to a vanishing point directly under the eclipsed Sun, with **one army on each bank**. Neither side is "left" or "right" of history. | Totality there needs ΔT about 300–400 s above the NASA value (§1.5). Say "the Halys", not "the exact spot". (The Kırıkkale–Kalecik reach runs north and does not give this alignment.) |
| **B. The plateau inside the computed band** (the Konya–Aksaray–Tuz Gölü plain) | Certain totality, about 2–3½ minutes. Real landmarks: **Hasan Dağı** (3,253 m, a snow-streaked volcano) to the SE; the white **Tuz Gölü** salt flats to the WNW–NW, under the setting Sun. | Not on the river. Use for wide "strategy-board" shots and the shadow sweep. |

**Light** (the single most important correction to the TREATMENT's mental image)

- The Sun is low in the **WNW**: 19° at first contact, **8–9° at totality**, setting about 50 minutes later.
- Light rakes almost horizontally, warm gold-orange. Shadows are **6–7 times as long as the object** and point ESE.
- **If the armies face each other east–west** (Lydians arriving from the west, Medes from the east), the Medes look into the low Sun and are front-lit, and the Lydians have it at their backs and are rim-lit. Alternate coverage so each side gets its golden faces and its silhouettes. In option A both banks get the same side-light.
- When the light goes strange:
  - Colours drain toward metal-grey and the light cools.
  - Contrast rises and shadow edges sharpen.
  - The WNW sky darkens first.

**The river (Kızılırmak)**

- **Opaque red-brown, the colour of brick dust or milky cocoa**, from iron-rich clay. Late May is high water after the spring snowmelt: full, fast, turbid.
- Width tens of metres up to 100-plus in the middle course (before the modern dams).
- Banks of **red clay cut 2–5 m high**, gravel bars, side channels and shallows where a duel can happen knee-deep.
- **Do not make it easy to ford everywhere**: in 547 BC Croesus needed bridges or Thales' diversion channel.
- Brackish water (gypsum and halite upstream; §1.3): white salt crust on drying mud at the margins.

**Vegetation**

- **On the banks**
  - Reeds (*Phragmites*).
  - **Tamarisk** shrubs, possibly still in pink feathery bloom.
  - Willows and **broad-crowned native black and white poplars**.
  - **Oleaster**, with silver leaves and fragrant flowers in May.
- **On the plateau**
  - Rolling steppe at 900–1,100 m, green going gold.
  - **Feather grass (*Stipa*)** with long silky silver plumes that ripple in wind: the perfect visible "eclipse wind".
  - Silver-grey wormwood, thistles, **red poppies**.
  - Barley and wheat still green-gold.
  - Oak scrub on the hills.
- **Avoid** the pencil-thin columnar poplar rows of modern Anatolia; they are mostly modern plantations. **[S]**
- **Avoid** the iconic Cappadocian "fairy chimney" postcard look. Use generic eroded tuff and sandstone bluffs instead.

**Life**

- **Onagers.** Strabo (12.2.10) says the plain "round Garsauira and Lycaonia" was "grazed by wild asses." Garsauira is modern **Aksaray, inside the totality band**. A herd of onagers bolting across the steppe as darkness falls is historically grounded. **[V]**
- **Birds**
  - White storks nesting in May.
  - Swallows and swifts low over the water.
  - Crested larks singing over the steppe, falling silent at totality.
  - Jackdaws and crows flocking to roost.
- Sheep, goats and their dogs.

**Sky**

- Late May is the plateau's wettest month, so **broken cumulus or a distant thunderhead lit gold from below** is plausible. It also hides the impossible-to-film partial Sun.
- During totality, see §5 and the sky chart.

**Army layout (invention; flag it as such)**

- Two lines facing each other across the water, or across a shallow ford.
  - **Each side:** infantry centre, cavalry wings, archers behind or on the flanks.
  - Median archers stand as a separate arm, per Cyaxares' reform (Herodotus 1.103).
- **Standards:** animal emblems on poles, never flags.
  - Lydian: a gold lion.
  - Median: a design of our own choosing. Bronze horse or winged-creature finials are safe; no attested Median standard survives.
- Camps behind each line, with smoke rising.

**Map to the worlds**

- **BRONZE:** Altdorfer aerial, umber steppe, red river, poplars.
- **MARBLE:** everything frozen, spray hanging over the red water like glass, feather-grass plumes stopped mid-wave.
- **Relief map:** the Landsat refs give true late-May colours and the shape of the Halys arc.

### 4.2 Thales' world: Miletus, c. 585 BC

**Geography then vs now**

- Archaic Miletus stood on a **peninsula reaching into the sea**: the Latmian Gulf, with the island of Lade offshore and four harbours.
- The Maeander's silt has since filled the gulf. **The ruins now stand about 8–10 km inland**, and Lake Bafa is the old gulf's remnant.
- For 585 BC, **put the sea at the city's feet.**

**The city**

- **Do not use the famous orthogonal street grid.** It belongs to the city rebuilt after the Persians destroyed Miletus in 494 BC.
- Archaic Miletus had irregular streets and houses of mudbrick on stone footings, plastered and lime-washed. They had flat roof terraces or low terracotta-tiled roofs.
- Sanctuaries:
  - Athena's temple.
  - The **Delphinion** of Apollo by the harbour.
  - Aphrodite's sanctuary on Zeytintepe.
- **Didyma**, the oracle of Apollo, lies about 16 km south along a **Sacred Way lined with archaic seated marble figures** (c. 600–550 BC; several are in the British Museum). These are ideal MARBLE-world citizens of Thales' generation.
- **Avoid** the Lion Harbour's marble lions; they are Hellenistic.

**Economy, usable as set dressing**

- **Wool and textiles**: Milesian wool was proverbial. Show looms, dyed hanks and purple dye-works.
- **Olive oil**: Thales' presses (§2.3), with stone beam presses and big storage jars.
- **Ships**: Miletus founded dozens of Black Sea colonies, Sinope among them, and traded with Egypt through Naukratis.
- **Pottery**: Wild Goat style and Fikellura (`greek_ionian/met_239950…`).

**Thales' workplace (designer-safe invention)**

- A courtyard house with a **flat roof terrace used as an observatory**.
- On the terrace:
  - A **gnomon** with its shadow ticked on the plaster.
  - A **polished bronze basin of water or oil** for watching the Sun.
- Wax tablets with **tallies of months**, a papyrus roll, an olive branch.
- At night: an **open saucer-type terracotta oil lamp** with a pinched wick-spout. Closed moulded lamps are later.
- **Sky:** the **Little Bear** (his constellation for sailors). Remember there was no pole star then (§1.6).
- **Lighting key:** La Tour's single flame (`painting/met_436839…`) for night; Rembrandt's warm gold (`thales/met_437394…`) for day.
- **Avoid** in his study: armillary spheres, globes, astrolabes, codices, glass windows, candles in candlesticks (use oil lamps).

**Colour.** Lime-white walls, terracotta roofs, red and black painted temple terracottas, bronze and olive green. The sea is a palette problem ("no blue until Earth"). Stage Miletus at **dawn or dusk**, when the sea reads gold or umber, or at night.

## 5. Eclipse visual checklist

Each item has one drawable line, then the specifics for 28 May 585 BC: low Sun in the WNW, observer facing it.

**Clock-face convention:** sky positions are given as a clock face on the solar disc, with 12 = up and 3 = right (= north when facing WNW). All positions are from our DE422 run; they shift slightly with where you stand in the band.

| # | Phenomenon | When | Draw it like this | 585 BC specifics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The bite** | C1 to C2, about 56 min | A crisp black notch eats the Sun; the sky stays bright almost to the end. | **First contact at about 5 o'clock (lower right).** The Moon slides up and to the left. Late in the partial phase the **bright crescent sits at about 11 o'clock, horns pointing down-right**. After C3 it is mirrored: **crescent at about 5 o'clock, horns up-left**. The bitten Sun is unfilmable naked-eye. Show it through thin cloud, haze, a reflection in water or oil (Seneca), or as pinhole images. |
| 2 | **"Light went strange"** | Beyond about 70 % | The world dims less than you expect but turns flat, cool and metallic. Colours drain. Faces look grey-silver. | With the Sun already gold and low, the shift reads as **warm gold draining to steel**. Use it for the verse-1 lyric. |
| 3 | **Sharpening shadows** | Beyond about 80 % | Shadows go razor-crisp, especially edges running parallel to the crescent's horns. | Shadows are already **6–7 times object height, pointing ESE**. Spear and poplar shadows become long crisp blades across the steppe. |
| 4 | **Pinhole crescents** | Partial phases | Every gap in the leaves projects a tiny crescent Sun, hundreds overlapping. | Projections are **flipped top to bottom** (viewed with the Sun at your back): before totality the bright edge sits at **about 7 o'clock**, after it at **about 1 o'clock**. **On flat ground they are stretched 4–6 times into slivers** along the shadow direction, because the Sun is low. On a **vertical surface facing the Sun** (a raised shield, a cliff, a tent wall) they are round-bodied crescents. **Median wicker shields are natural pinhole arrays**: a shield's weave throws a constellation of crescents onto the Lydian beside it. |
| 5 | **Jupiter appears** | Last minutes before C2 | One brilliant point pops out near the Sun before any star. | **Jupiter (−1.8), 11° above and 5.5° left of the Sun.** Not Venus: Venus was below the horizon. |
| 6 | **The shadow comes** | About 30 s before C2 | A dark wall rises over the horizon and races toward you; the whole sky changes in seconds. | **It comes out of the sunset**, from the WNW under and around the Sun, at **about 5 km/s (18,000 km/h)**, and leaves toward the ESE behind you. It crosses the visible landscape in seconds. |
| 7 | **Shadow bands** | 1–2 min either side of totality | Faint, fast, wavy ripples of light and dark, a few cm apart, sliding over pale surfaces like light on a pool floor. | Show them on **white linen** (Thales' chiton, a tent), pale dust, a marble plinth. Low contrast: never strobe it. |
| 8 | **Baily's beads** | The last 2–3 s before C2; the first 2–3 s after C3 | The last sunlight breaks into a string of brilliant beads along one edge of the black disc: light through lunar valleys. | **C2 beads near the top of the disc** (about 11–1 o'clock). **C3 beads at lower right** (about 3–5 o'clock). |
| 9 | **Diamond ring** | One bead plus inner corona, about 1–2 s | A single blinding point on a thin pearly ring: a jewel on a ring of light. | C2 ring near the top. **C3 ring, "a sudden spark", at lower right.** The light returns from the WNW. |
| 10 | **Chromosphere and prominences** | First and last seconds of totality | A thin **pink-red (hydrogen-alpha) rim** flashes on the limb. Small **pink tongues and arches** stand off the edge in a few places. | The Moon over-covers the Sun by about 1′ (33.5′ vs 31.4′), so the pink shows best **at the contact points**: top at C2, lower right at C3. |
| 11 | **Corona** | Totality | Soft pearly-white streamers, brightest close in, fading outward. Silky, not spiky; no rays like a cartoon sun. | The 585 BC solar cycle is unknown, so the shape is our call. **Solar-minimum type** (two long wings with short polar brushes): the wings would stand **almost upright, top leaning about 20° left**, since the solar equator was tilted about 71° from horizontal. **Solar-maximum type**: a round "iris" of streamers. Because the Sun is only 8.4° up (about 6.6 air masses), the corona looks **smaller, warmer and dimmer** than high-Sun photographs. |
| 12 | **The black disc** | Totality | A perfectly black, hard-edged disc; blacker than any sky. | Hangs low over the WNW horizon, near Jupiter, with Pollux and Castor about 19° above. |
| 13 | **360° horizon glow** | Totality | A sunset in every direction: an orange-yellow band all round the horizon under a dark dome. | **Brightest to the NNE and SSW** (the nearest edges of the long, narrow shadow); **darker under the Sun (WNW) and opposite it (ESE)**. Zenith: deep slate-indigo. In our palette, render it as near-black umber-violet. |
| 14 | **How dark** | Totality | Deep twilight: faces readable at arm's length, colours mostly gone. Bright planets and stars out. | Visible: Jupiter, Saturn (high SW), Capella, Procyon, Pollux, Arcturus, Vega; probably Regulus, Spica and Castor. Not Orion, not Sirius. |
| 15 | **Temperature drop, eclipse wind** | From deep partial to just after totality | People pull cloaks close; a cool breath of wind; dust settles; banners stir then hang. | Expect a few °C (the Sun was already low and weak). Show the **feather-grass plumes rippling** and horses' manes lifting. |
| 16 | **Animals** | The last minutes before and during totality | Birdsong stops. Swallows and swifts drop low and vanish; crows and jackdaws flock to roost; crickets start; dogs bark; horses fidget. | Crested larks fall silent over the steppe; storks glide to nests; **onagers bolt across the plain** (Strabo; §4.1). This is the lyric "Birds went quiet". |
| 17 | **Human reaction** | Throughout | Hush, then cries, prayers, people kneeling or looking away, a few staring. When the light returns: **a roar**. | Herodotus: they **stopped fighting**. Make the stop a choice, staggered person by person, not a freeze. |
| 18 | **The return** | After C3 | Sudden flood of low, warm light from the WNW; long shadows snap back; the corona vanishes in a second. | About 50 minutes of real **golden hour** follow. Toward the eastern plateau the **Sun sets still bitten**: a crescent sinking into the haze with its upper left missing. |

**Common mistakes to avoid**

- A visible "bite" with the naked eye in a bright sky.
- Rays radiating from the corona.
- A corona that is blue or green.
- Stars all over the sky during partial phases.
- Totality lasting "minutes of gradual dusk". The plunge from C2 is abrupt: about 30 seconds from bright twilight to night.
- A crescent Moon visible near the Sun (the Moon *is* the black disc).
- Venus.

**The best palette precedent of all.** Pliny (*Natural History* 35.32 in the Bostock edition, §35.50 in modern numbering) says Apelles and the great Greek painters worked **with four colours only**: "melinum for the white, Attic sil for the yellow, **Pontic sinopis for the red**, and atramentum for the black." The editor notes, "Blue is here excluded altogether." **[V]**

- Strabo adds that the prized "**Sinopean**" red ochre "**is produced in Cappadocia**" and only took its name from the port it was shipped through (12.2.10). **[V]**
- So the TREATMENT's no-blue BRONZE world is the classical painter's own palette, and its red is **literally the earth of the Halys country**.

### 8.2 Astronomy (all numbers in §1 come from our own runs unless marked)

**Ephemeris and software**

- **JPL DE422** (−3000 to +3000), from the PyPI `de422` package (jplephem format), evaluated with our Chebyshev adapter inside **Skyfield 1.55**.
- Corrections applied: aberration and light deflection. The Moon's radius uses k = 0.2722810 for the umbra and 0.2724880 for penumbral contacts.
- Topocentric observers sit on the WGS84 ellipsoid; refraction is included for the horizon.

**Validation**

- 21 Aug 2017 total eclipse. NASA: 2 m 40.2 s at 18:25:32 UT, Sun at 64°. Ours: 2 m 40 s, 18:25:31, 63.9°.
