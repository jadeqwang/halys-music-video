# What Herodotus tells us, and how the ancients predicted eclipses

*HALYS* is built on one paragraph of Herodotus. This page covers what that paragraph says, what nobody knows, how ancient eclipse prediction worked, and where the film bends the record. Full sourcing: [RESEARCH.md](RESEARCH.md), [FACTCHECK.md](FACTCHECK.md), [research/THALES_METHOD.md](research/THALES_METHOD.md).

## 1. What Herodotus tells us

Herodotus wrote around 430 BC, about 150 years after the battle; the story is in Book 1 of his *Histories*, chapters 73–75. Quotations are from A. D. Godley's translation (Loeb, 1920).

**How the war started.** Scythians serving Cyaxares, king of the Medes, came back from a hunt empty-handed, and he treated them "very roughly and contemptuously". In revenge they served him a murdered boy as game and fled to Alyattes, king of Lydia, at Sardis. Alyattes refused to hand them over (1.73).

**Five years, then the sixth.** "There was war between the Lydians and the Medes for five years; each won many victories over the other, and once they fought a battle by night." Then:

> "They were still warring with equal success, when it happened, at an encounter which occurred in the sixth year, that during the battle the day was suddenly turned to night. Thales of Miletus had foretold this loss of daylight to the Ionians, fixing it within the year in which the change did indeed happen." (1.74.2)

**The peace.** "So when the Lydians and Medes saw the day turned to night, they stopped fighting, and both were the more eager to make peace." Two outsiders, Syennesis of Cilicia and Labynetus of Babylon, brokered a sworn agreement and a royal marriage: Alyattes' daughter Aryenis would marry Astyages, son of Cyaxares, "for without strong constraint agreements will not keep their force" (1.74.3–4).

**The oath.** "These nations make sworn compacts as do the Greeks; and besides, when they cut the skin of their arms, they lick each other's blood." (1.74.5)

**The border.** The river Halys, today's Kızılırmak ("Red River"), was "the boundary of the Median and Lydian empires" (1.72). A generation later Alyattes' son Croesus crossed it to attack Cyrus. Most Greeks said Thales got the army across by digging a channel that split the river; Herodotus himself believed there were bridges (1.75).

### What is uncertain

| Question | What we can say |
|---|---|
| Which eclipse? | **28 May 585 BC** (Julian calendar; 22 May in today's). Of all solar eclipses visible in western Anatolia from 620 to 560 BC, only this one comes close to "day turned to night": total at Sardis, 97 % at Miletus (percentages on this page are of the Sun's diameter). The old rivals (610, 603, 582 BC) were partial, at about 50–90 % ([RESEARCH §1.2](RESEARCH.md); Stephenson & Fatoohi 1997). |
| What time of day? | Late afternoon, about 18:20 local solar time: the Sun 8–10° above the west-north-west horizon, under an hour before sunset. Totality lasted 2 to 3½ minutes across central Anatolia. |
| Where was the battle? | Unknown. Herodotus never says it was at the Halys; "Battle of the Halys" is a modern label. |
| Was it total there? | Totality swept Sardis, the Konya plain and Tarsus. The Halys itself got 96–99 %, unless the slowing of Earth's rotation (ΔT) was about 300 seconds off the standard value, well within the uncertainty ([RESEARCH §1.5](RESEARCH.md)). |
| Did Thales really predict it? | Herodotus says so, 150 years later, and only to the year. No ancient source says how. See §3. |

## 2. How the ancients predicted eclipses

**Why eclipses repeat.** The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Sun's path, so an eclipse needs a new moon (solar) or a full moon (lunar) near one of the two points where the paths cross. Those windows come round about every six months, sometimes five. After 223 lunar months (18 years, 10 or 11 days and 8 hours), Sun, Moon and crossing points line up almost exactly as before, and the eclipse repeats. In those extra 8 hours Earth turns a third of the way round, so the next solar eclipse in a series falls about 120° of longitude further west.

**Lunar eclipses are easy; solar eclipses are hard.** A lunar eclipse is visible from the whole night side of Earth, so "eclipse this month" works wherever it is night. A solar eclipse is total only along a narrow track, so "possible this month" says almost nothing about one city.

| When | Who | How | Could it say *where* a solar eclipse would be seen? |
|---|---|---|---|
| From 747 BC | Babylonian scribes | They kept eclipse records and flagged possibilities by counting months: 6 apart, sometimes 5. They used the 223-month cycle for lunar eclipses by the 6th century BC (Steele 2000). | No |
| 7th c. BC | Assyrian court scholars | They forecast eclipse "possibilities", watched for them, and reported the ones that "passed" unseen (Steele 2026). | No |
| From about 400 BC | Babylonian mathematical astronomy | Arithmetic schemes for the Moon's changing speed gave eclipse dates, times and sizes (Ossendrijver 2012). | No: it had no geography (Neugebauer 1957) |
| 2nd c. BC | Hipparchus | Geometric models of Sun and Moon. He measured the Moon's distance from an eclipse that was total near the Hellespont but only four-fifths at Alexandria. | In principle |
| 2nd–1st c. BC | The Antikythera mechanism | The 223-month cycle is cut into bronze gears, and a spiral dial marks the months of possible lunar and solar eclipses, with their hours (Freeth 2014). | It lists possibilities |
| c. AD 150 | Ptolemy, *Almagest* VI | A full procedure, including parallax, for how much of the Sun is covered in each latitude zone | Roughly |
| 1715 | Edmond Halley | Working from the best lunar tables of his day, he published a map of the shadow's path across England before the eclipse. | Yes |

**So was it brute-force pattern matching?** At first, mostly yes. Babylonian prediction was a search for periodicities in a carefully kept log, closer to fitting periods to a long time series than to physics, and it worked well for the Moon. By about 400 BC it had become algorithmic: arithmetic tables modelled the Moon's changing speed, still with no picture of the sky's geometry. The Greeks added sizes, distances and parallax. That geometry is what turns "possible this month" into "visible from here".

**The verdict for 585 BC.** At most, someone could have said an eclipse was possible in a given month or year. No method anywhere could have said it would be total over Lydia, at sunset, on 28 May. A forecast "to the year" is what a rule for spotting possibilities gives you; a visible hit took luck.

## 3. Thales specifically

**What the sources say.**
- Herodotus gives only the year.
- Eudemus of Rhodes (c. 320 BC), quoted by Diogenes Laertius and by Clement, calls him the first to predict eclipses of the sun.
- Cicero (44 BC) writes that "he is said to have been the first man to predict the solar eclipse".
- Pliny (AD 77) dates it to the fourth year of the 48th Olympiad, 585/584 BC.

None of them says how.

**The leading hypotheses.** None is attested.

| Idea | Who | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| A Babylonian cycle: 585 falls exactly 223 months after an eclipse on 18 May 603 BC | The traditional view | We compute that the 603 eclipse was total across Arabia, the northern Persian Gulf and Iran, and about 85 % at Babylon, but at Miletus it covered only half the Sun's width, in mid-morning. Babylonian records would have had to reach Thales, and they would have given a possibility, not visibility. |
| Patterns seen from Miletus: eclipses in threes, the second 17–18 months after the first, the third at 35 | Dirk Couprie (2004, 2011) | Our scan finds the pattern in 610–607 and 588–585 BC; it breaks after 585, and it depends on people noticing partial eclipses. |
| A solar eclipse 23½ months after a lunar one | Patricia O'Grady (2002), after van der Waerden | Couprie puts its success rate at no more than 23 %. |
| A 54-year cycle | Dmitri Panchenko (1994) | It points to 582 BC, an eclipse that never darkened Asia Minor (Stephenson & Fatoohi 1997). |
| Luck, or a story that grew | Otto Neugebauer: "there exists no cycle for solar eclipses visible at a given place". D. R. Dicks (1959) argued that Thales could not have had the knowledge. | Miguel Querejeta (2011) tested the proposed cycles and found none of them satisfactory. |

**The 1/720 claim.** Diogenes Laertius (3rd c. AD) says that "according to some" Thales declared the Sun to be 1/720 of its circle, and the Moon the same fraction of its own. Both would then look half a degree wide, which is why the Moon can exactly cover the Sun. It is not a prediction method: it explains how a total eclipse is possible, not when one will happen. The attribution is shaky too. Apuleius (2nd c. AD), the earliest writer to credit Thales with measuring the Sun, gives no number and puts the discovery in Thales' old age, long after 585. Archimedes, writing centuries earlier, credits the 1/720 to Aristarchus.

## 4. What the film shows, and the liberties it takes

On-screen facts are checked in [FACTCHECK.md](FACTCHECK.md). Where the film departs from the record:

- **The battle, and totality, at the river.** Herodotus does not say where the battle was fought; the film puts it on the Halys, the border. Under the standard ΔT the Halys saw 96–99 %, which is not night. Jade's terminal admits it: `dt.shift(+300)` nudges the shadow north, so the river gets 1 m 19 s of totality.
- **The sky is real.** The late-afternoon light, the Sun 9° up in the west-north-west, Jupiter beside the black Sun, and totality about 56 minutes after first contact are all computed from JPL ephemerides ([RESEARCH §1.4–1.6](RESEARCH.md)).
- **Laying down arms.** Herodotus says they stopped fighting and wanted peace. The dropped swords and the walk home are our staging.
- **The oath.** Herodotus describes cut arms and shared blood. The film shows the kings clasping right hands, each forearm with a thin fresh cut, the mediators behind. The clasp is a Greek pledge, the kings swearing in person is our inference, and nobody licks anything.
- **Faces.** No portrait of Thales, Alyattes or Cyaxares survives, so the faces are invented; the costumes are researched ([RESEARCH §3](RESEARCH.md)). Thales is about 40, not an old sage.
- **Thales' year.** Herodotus says only that Thales named the year, and that is all the gold figure beside him claims. It is an arc of twelve new moons between two limit strokes (Herodotus' word is οὖρος, "limit"), with the Sun going dark inside it. The plaque reads HE NAMED ONLY THE YEAR · HERODOTUS 1.74. The dim lines around him are things later writers credited him with: the triangle in a semicircle, and measuring a pyramid by its shadow. They are ornament, not how he foretold the eclipse; nobody knows that ([research note](research/THALES_METHOD.md)).
- **The simulation.** Jade scheduling the eclipse from her terminal is, of course, fiction.
